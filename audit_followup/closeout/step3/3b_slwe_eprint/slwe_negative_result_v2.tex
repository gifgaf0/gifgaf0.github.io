\title{A rank collapse in a sedenion Module-LWE proposal,\\ and why a uniform matrix does not repair it}
\author{Matthew Gifford\\ \small Independent researcher, Hollister, California, USA}
\date{\small October 2026 (revised)}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We report a negative result on SLWE, a Module-LWE variant over the 16-dimensional sedenion algebra that the
author proposed in an earlier paper with a claim of NIST Category~5 security. Its public matrix is built from orbits of an order-7 coordinate permutation (a
``Singer cycle''). First, the flattened public matrix factors through a fixed $256\times112$ integer matrix of rank 76,
so its rank over $\F_q$ is at most 76 for every module rank $k$, and exactly 76 for generic seeds once $k\ge 7$, whereas a
uniform matrix of the same shape has rank $16k$. This invalidates the security claim at both published parameter sets.
Recovering the noise then reduces to an LWE instance of dimension 76, which the lattice estimator places at about
$2^{39}$ operations at the first set ($n=512$, $q\approx2^{32}$) and $2^{40}$ at the second ($n=1024$, $q=911$). Second,
replacing the matrix by a uniform one does not restore the claim (centred binomial noise with $\eta=2$, ternary secrets
of weight 64): the estimator gives about $2^{51}$ at the first set and about $2^{167}$ at the second, against roughly
$2^{272}$ for Category~5. Third, decryption at the first set never fails: the noise is bounded by 258, far below $q/4$.
All computations are reproducible from public scripts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

SLWE was proposed inside a larger, physics-motivated programme that uses the sedenions $\Sed$ (the Cayley--Dickson
double of the octonions) and their zero divisors. The idea was to keep the shape of Module-LWE~\cite{Regev,LS15} while replacing the ring by
$\Sed_q=\Sed\otimes\F_q$ and to give the public matrix a symmetry inherited from the Fano plane: each row is the orbit of
a random seed under a cyclic permutation $\sigma$ of order 7. Correctness was obtained with a bilinear ``norm'' pairing
rather than the (non-associative) algebra product.

Both versions of the paper that proposed SLWE~\cite{Gif26a} claimed NIST Category~5 security. Version~1 used $n=512$
and $q\approx2^{32}$. Version~2 found that modulus far too large (its primal estimate put version~1 below Category~1),
moved to $k=64$ and $q=911$ ($n=1024$), and claimed Category~5 from a primal estimate, leaving the dual and hybrid
attacks to be checked; Section~\ref{sec:uniform} checks them. It kept the Singer-orbit matrix, whose rank it had tested only at $k=4$. The project's working
specification~\cite{Gif26b} recorded on May~10, 2026 that this matrix is rank-deficient at $k=32$, and replaced it by a
uniform one.

This note shows that neither version is secure and records three facts that may be useful beyond this proposal.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A coordinate permutation applied to seeds produces a matrix that is linear in the seeds through a fixed
  ``design matrix''. The rank of that design matrix bounds the rank of the public matrix for every module rank
  (Section~\ref{sec:rank}).
\item Non-associativity of the algebra plays no role once decryption uses a bilinear form: every map the attacker needs
  is $\F_q$-linear on $\F_q^{16k}$.
\item At $q\approx2^{32}$ with noise of standard deviation about 1, LWE in dimension 512 is easy whatever the matrix
  (Section~\ref{sec:uniform}). The modulus-to-noise ratio, not the algebra, governs security. Version~2
  of~\cite{Gif26a} reached the same conclusion with a primal estimate; Section~\ref{sec:uniform} confirms it with the
  standard estimator.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The scheme}\label{sec:scheme}

Let $\Sed_q$ be the sedenions over $\F_q$ with basis $e_0,\dots,e_{15}$, built by four Cayley--Dickson doublings
$(a,b)(c,d)=(ac-\bar d b,\ da+b\bar c)$. Write $L(a)\in\F_q^{16\times16}$ for left multiplication by $a$ and
$\langle u,v\rangle=\re(\bar u v)=\sum_t u_t v_t$ for the norm pairing, extended to $\Sed_q^k$ by summing over
components. The identity $L(a)^{\mathsf T}=L(\bar a)$ gives $\langle A s,r\rangle=\langle s,A^{H}r\rangle$ with
$A^{H}_{ij}=\overline{A_{ji}}$, which is what decryption needs.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{KeyGen.} $s\in\Sed_q^k$ is sparse ternary (weight $h_s$). $A\in\Sed_q^{k\times k}$ has
  $A_{ij}=\sigma^{j}(\mathrm{seed}_i)$ with independent uniform seeds, where $\sigma$ moves the coefficient of $e_t$ to
  $e_{(t\bmod7)+1}$ for $t=1..7$, moves that of $e_{t+8}$ to $e_{(t\bmod7)+9}$, and fixes $e_0,e_8$. The noise $e$ is
  centred binomial (or confined to the four-dimensional kernel of a zero divisor~\cite{Moreno}). The public key is $(A,\ b=As+e)$.
\item \textbf{Encaps.} $r$ is sparse ternary (weight $h_r$), with $c_1=A^{H}r+e_1$ and
  $c_2=\langle b,r\rangle+e_2+m\lfloor q/2\rceil$.\footnote{For odd $q$, $\lfloor q/2\rceil$ stands for $\lfloor q/2\rfloor$ or $\lceil q/2\rceil$; the
  reference code uses $\lfloor q/2\rfloor$. Section~\ref{sec:correct} treats both.}
\item \textbf{Decaps.} Round $c_2-\langle s,c_1\rangle=m\lfloor q/2\rceil+N$ with
  $N=\langle e,r\rangle-\langle s,e_1\rangle+e_2$.
\end{itemize}
Version~1 specifies $n=512$ (so $k=32$), a modulus $q\approx2^{32}$ with $q\equiv1\pmod{455}$, and sparse ternary
secrets. We instantiate it with the least prime above $2^{32}$ that is $\equiv1\pmod{455}$, $q=4\,294\,977\,961$, and
with $h_s=h_r=64$ and CBD$(\eta=2)$ noise, the values of version~2 and of the project's ledger~\cite{Led}. Version~2
specifies $k=64$ ($n=1024$), $q=911$ (the least prime $\equiv1\pmod{455}$), $h_s=h_r=64$ and CBD$(2)$ noise.

\section{The rank collapse}\label{sec:rank}

Let $M\in\F_q^{16k\times16k}$ be the matrix of $s\mapsto As$, i.e.\ $M_{16i+u,\,16j+c}=$ coefficient $u$ of
$\sigma^{j}(\mathrm{seed}_i)\,e_c$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rank}
Let $N\in\mathbb{Z}^{256\times112}$ have entries $N_{16m+u,\,16j+c}=$ coefficient $u$ of $\sigma^{j}(e_m)\,e_c$
($0\le m,u,c\le15$, $0\le j\le6$). Then $M=(S\otimes I_{16})\,N_k$, where $S\in\F_q^{k\times16}$ holds the seeds and
$N_k$ consists of the column blocks $j\bmod 7$ of $N$, $j=0,\dots,k-1$. Hence $\rank M\le\rank N$ for every $k$.
Moreover $\rank_{\mathbb{Q}}N=76$, and $\rank N=76$ modulo $911$ and modulo $q$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The product is bilinear and $\sigma$ is linear, so $\sigma^{j}(\mathrm{seed}_i)\,e_c=\sum_m(\mathrm{seed}_i)_m\,
\sigma^{j}(e_m)\,e_c$. That is the stated factorisation. Since $\sigma^7=\mathrm{id}$, column block $j$ of $N_k$ equals
block $j\bmod7$, so $N_k$ has at most the columns of $N$. The ranks of $N$ were computed exactly (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).
\end{proof}

The bound is attained. Table~\ref{tab:rank} lists exact ranks from two independent implementations (one built on the
proposal's reference code, one written blind from a textual specification), with three seed sets per case and a
uniform control. The collapse starts at $k=5$; from $k=7$ on the rank is 76 for every seed set tried. Version~2's
parameters, $k=64$ over $\F_{911}$, are in the table: the rank is 76, not 1024. Its own rank test used $k=4$, where the
matrix has 64 columns, below the cap of 76, so the collapse could not show there.

\begin{remark}
$\sigma$ is not an automorphism of the sedenion product, so the ``symmetry'' of $A$ is a relabelling of coordinates. The
argument above uses only linearity.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}
Let $V$ be the column space of $M$ ($\dim V=\rho\le76$). Then $e\in b+V$. Choose $\rho$ coordinates $I$ on which a basis
of $V$ is invertible. Then $e_{\bar I}=-C e_I+(b_{\bar I}+C b_I)$ for a public $C$, an LWE instance with secret $e_I$ of
dimension $\rho$ and $16k-\rho$ samples. The same holds for $e_1$ and the column space of $M^{H}$. Given $e$ and $e_1$, any
$r'$ with $M^{H}r'=c_1-e_1$ gives $c_2-\langle b-e,r'\rangle=m\lfloor q/2\rceil+\langle e,r\rangle+e_2$, which reveals $m$.
\end{corollary}

For $\rho=76$, $q=4\,294\,977\,961$, CBD$(2)$ secret and noise and $m=436$ samples, the lattice
estimator~\cite{APS15,estimator} reports $2^{38.6}$ operations (uSVP and BDD). Its block size is at the floor of its
model ($\beta=40$), so reduction at the scale of LLL already suffices. Distinguishing $(A,b)$ from uniform is easier
still: it needs only Gaussian elimination, because $A$ itself is rank-deficient. At version~2's parameters the same
reduction gives dimension 76 with 948 samples, and the estimator reports $2^{40.4}$ (BDD hybrid, again at $\beta=40$).

\section{A uniform matrix does not restore the claimed security}\label{sec:uniform}

After the rank deficiency was found, the project replaced the Singer matrix by a uniform one. Brief~06~\cite{Brief06}
adds a uniform perturbation to it, which at the strength chosen there ($\delta=1$) makes the matrix uniform, and puts it
into the key generation of the project's hybrid-KEM wrapper, whose target is $k=32$ with $q\approx2^{32}$~\cite{KEM}.
The working specification~\cite{Gif26b} states that its security table applies with this matrix. Table~\ref{tab:est}
shows the estimator's default attack set with a uniform matrix at version~1's setting as instantiated in
Section~\ref{sec:scheme}, at the two SLWE rows of the security table of~\cite{Gif26b}, at $q=2731$, and at version~2's
parameters. At version~1's setting the cheapest attack costs about $2^{51}$. An
independent implementation of the primal ``2016 estimate''~\cite{ADPS16} (smallest $\beta$ with
$\sigma\sqrt\beta\le\delta_\beta^{2\beta-d-1}\mathrm{Vol}^{1/d}$, the secret rescaled to the noise width) gives $\beta=72$,
$d=937$, a core-SVP cost of $2^{0.292\beta}=2^{21}$~\cite{BDGL16}, the same as the estimator's own rough uSVP figure.
The cause is the modulus: with $q/\sigma\approx2^{32}$, a block size near 70 already exposes the short vector in
dimension about 940. A dense ternary secret instead of weight 64 changes little: about $2^{53}$.

The working specification~\cite{Gif26b} targets $n=512$ with ``$\sim$3329 (mod-455 prime)''. Its security table
labels $q=911$ ``L5'' (408 bits classical) and $q=3329$ ``L3'' (341 bits), from a formula calibrated on Kyber that
assumes a full-rank matrix. With weight-64 secrets neither row reaches $2^{128}$: $q=911$ gives at most about $2^{127}$
and $q=3329$ about $2^{112}$. A dense ternary secret lifts $q=911$ only to about $2^{149}$. The ML-KEM modulus 3329 is
not $\equiv1\pmod{455}$; the nearest prime below it that is, 2731, gives about $2^{114}$. At $n=512$, then, even the
smallest admissible prime gives at most about $2^{127}$ with weight-64 secrets and CBD$(2)$ noise.

Version~2 took a larger dimension. With a uniform matrix, the estimator's cheapest attack on its parameters is the
primal hybrid with a meet-in-the-middle step, at about $2^{167}$; without that step the hybrid costs $2^{181}$. The other
lattice attacks cost $2^{239}$ (dual hybrid), $2^{249}$ (BDD), $2^{254}$ (primal uSVP, the attack version~2 used, at
$\beta=800$ against its claimed $\beta\approx942$) and $2^{268}$ (dual). NIST's thresholds are about $2^{143}$ classical
gates for Category~1, $2^{207}$ for Category~3 and $2^{272}$ for Category~5~\cite{NIST16}; version~2 measured its own
gate count against the last. Reading the estimator's operation counts as gate counts, every lattice attack in the
default set is below $2^{272}$, so version~2's Category~5 claim fails even with a uniform matrix, and the primal hybrid
attacks put its parameters below Category~3. We have not searched the space of $q$, $\eta$ and secret weight further.

\section{Correctness}\label{sec:correct}

Each coordinate of $e$, $e_1$ and $e_2$ lies in $[-2,2]$. The confined zero-divisor noise has the same bound, since the
kernel bases have entries in $\{0,\pm1\}$ and the noise is $2\sum_j\alpha_jk_j$ with $\alpha_j\in\{-1,0,1\}$. So
\[
|N|\le 2h_r+2h_s+2=258<\lfloor q/4\rfloor\approx1.07\times10^{9},
\]
and decryption never fails at version~1's setting. The bound $258<q/4$ alone would suggest the same for every $q>1032$,
but at the boundary the half unit lost in rounding $q/2$ matters: at $q=1033$, a prime, the worst case $|N|=258$ decodes
wrongly with either rounding of $q/2$ and under every decoding rule we tried (thresholds at $q/4$ and $3q/4$, the
nearest point, rounding $2v/q$, and the reference code's branchless rounding). From $q=1036$ on it never fails under any
of them, so the first prime at which decryption never fails is 1039. At version~2's $q=911$ it can fail, though rarely.
For CBD noise the distribution is in fact exact: given $r$ and $s$, $N$ is a sum of $2(h_r+h_s+1)$ independent symmetric
binomial draws, i.e.\ $N\sim\mathrm{CBD}(258)$. At $q=911$ the tail is $\Pr[|N|>227]=2^{-353.5}$. The failure rate
itself, for a uniform message bit, depends on the rounding at the boundary: it is $2^{-353.9}$ for every rule and
rounding above except the reference code's decoder with its encoding $m\lfloor q/2\rfloor$, which gives $2^{-351.4}$.
Both are consistent with version~2's Gaussian bound of $2^{-295}$.

The project's working ledger~\cite{Led} reported $\log_2\mathrm{DFR}\approx-6.4\times10^{15}$ for version~1's setting.
That number is the Gaussian tail at
$z=q/(4\sigma_N)\approx9.5\times10^{7}$ with $\sigma_N\approx11.4$. It evaluates the tail about $4\times10^{6}$ times
beyond the largest value $N$ can take. At that modulus the correct statement is $\mathrm{DFR}=0$.

\section{Conclusion}

The Singer-orbit public matrix makes SLWE insecure at every module rank, including both published parameter sets. A
uniform matrix does not restore the claimed security: version~1's setting reaches about $2^{51}$, and version~2's
parameters about $2^{167}$, below Category~5 under every lattice attack in the estimator's default set. The scheme's correctness
was never in question. The general lesson is
elementary but easy to miss. A structured matrix built by acting on random seeds is the image of a fixed design matrix,
and that matrix's rank should be the first thing checked.
