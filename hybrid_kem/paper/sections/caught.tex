\subsection{In the proof sketch}\label{sec:sketchcaught}

Before the mechanization, the repository contained a reduction-based proof
sketch in a Bellare--Rogaway-style model~\cite{BR93,CK01}. It had two
theorems for the protocol and two lemmas for the hybrid primitives. The
theorems survived, with different bounds. Both lemmas were wrong as stated.

\paragraph{The composite signature.} The sketch stated
$\Adv^{\text{euf-cma}}_{\text{composite}} \le \min(\Adv^{\text{euf-cma}}_{\text{ML-DSA}}, \Adv^{\text{euf-cma}}_{\text{EdDSA}})$,
arguing that a composite forgery on a fresh message $M^*$ contains, for each
component, a valid signature on $M^*$ that the component never produced. The
components do not sign $M^*$, though. They sign $M'$, which depends on $M^*$
only through $\PH(M^*)$. If $\PH(M^*) = \PH(M)$ for a message $M$ signed
earlier under the same context, the composite signature on $M$ is also a
valid signature on $M^*$. Modelling the draft's message representative
exactly made the missing assumption unavoidable: the proof needs collision
resistance of $\PH$, and the corrected bound is \cref{lem:sig}. The mutant
\ident{composite_no_prehash_collision_resistance} confirms that the
proof fails without it. With SHA-512 or SHAKE256 as $\PH$, the extra term is
negligible in practice, but the sketch's claim that the composite is as
strong as its stronger component holds only together with a hash
assumption.

\paragraph{X-Wing.} The sketch stated, for both profiles,
$\Adv^{\text{ind-cca}}_{\text{KEM}} \le \min(\Adv^{\text{ind-cca}}_{\text{ML-KEM}}, \Adv^{\text{dh}}_{\text{X25519}}) + \varepsilon_{\text{comb}}$,
on the grounds that the combiner is a dual PRF. For X-Wing relying on
X25519, this misses C2PRI of ML-KEM. In the mechanized proof the assumption
is used at exactly one point: after the random-oracle step, it rules out
decapsulation queries $(\ct_M', \ct_X^*)$ with $\ct_M' \ne \ct_M^*$ that
would reach the challenge's oracle input (\cref{sec:mech:kem}). This is the
X-Wing paper's own classical theorem~\cite[Thm.~1]{XWing}, which bounds
X-Wing's IND-CCA advantage by $2\Delta_N$ plus the strong-DH and C2PRI
advantages. The X-Wing paper also proves that ML-KEM-768 is C2PRI when its
hash functions are random oracles~\cite[Thm.~3]{XWing}, so X-Wing's
guarantee survives a break of the lattice problem. But the ``either leg''
statement is not symmetric: the X25519 leg needs a property of the ML-KEM
leg. The ML-KEM-1024 profile, which hashes $\ct_M$, needs no property of
ML-KEM in its X25519 direction, so the sketch's symmetric form does hold for
it, once its unspecified Diffie--Hellman assumption is made precise as
strong Diffie--Hellman with a random oracle. The mutant
\ident{bbfgs_rom_no_ctM_in_combiner} shows that hashing $\ct_M$ is the
reason.

\subsection{In our write-up: an independent review}\label{sec:reviewcaught}

After the first version of the mechanization was merged, a separate instance
of the AI assistant reviewed it. It had the repository but not the
development history. It re-ran every model, probed the proofs, and found no
unsound step and no vacuous query. It did find places where the write-up
claimed more than the models prove. We re-checked each finding with
CryptoVerif before acting on it.

\paragraph{Tightness.} The first write-up said the mechanized bound had ``no
session-guessing loss'', so that the $n_S^2$ factors of the sketch's bound
were ``an artefact of the sketch's proof strategy''. The first half is true:
CryptoVerif's proof guesses no session. The conclusion is not. The KEM term
$\cvp{Pkemcca}(t, \calls{OI1}, \calls{OA1}+\calls{OR1}, \calls{OI2})$ is a
\emph{multi-key, multi-challenge} IND-CCA2 advantage, with $\calls{OI1}$
keys and up to $\calls{OR1} + \calls{OA1}$ challenges, since one $m_1$ can
be delivered to every responder. Reducing it to single-key, single-challenge
IND-CCA2 generically loses a factor of about keys times
challenges~\cite{BBM00}, that is, about $n_S^2$, the same order as the
sketch. What is better than the sketch is narrower. The signature term is
$N_K \cdot \Adv^{\text{euf-cma}}$ rather than $n_S^2 \cdot \Adv^{\text{euf-cma}}$,
and the PRF term is linear in the sessions. A tighter overall bound would
need a tight multi-user bound for the KEM, which we do not claim.

\paragraph{Non-standard assumptions not flagged.} The sketch justified ``HKDF
is a PRF keyed by its input keying material'' by HMAC being a
PRF~\cite{Kra10}. HKDF-Extract keys HMAC with the salt, however, so keying it
through the ikm uses HMAC's message input. That is the swap-PRF property,
which Backendal et al.\ prove only for feasible key sets~\cite{BBGS23}
(\cref{sec:results}). For the ML-KEM-1024 combiner, the first write-up stated
both legs' assumptions without saying that neither is standard: one is a
split-key PRF and the other a PRF-ODH variant (\cref{sec:mech:kem}). We now
name each assumption precisely, and we added the random-oracle proof from
standard assumptions for that combiner's X25519 leg.

\paragraph{Modelling of X25519.} The first write-up said the X-Wing models
make ``exactly the X-Wing paper's assumptions''. They do, up to one
difference: the models use a prime-order group and the paper a nominal
group. The review also noted that pyca/cryptography raises an error on an
all-zero X25519 output, while the models have no such failure; this led to
\cref{sec:lowcaught}.

\paragraph{The adversary was weaker than described.} In the first protocol
model, RevealKey refused to reveal a session's key whenever a \emph{possible}
partner existed, matching on $m_1$ alone, before the transcript was fixed.
So the adversary could not reveal some keys that the freshness definition
allows it to reveal. We replaced this with RevealKey on request for every
non-fresh session and partner-based freshness on the full transcript
(\cref{sec:freshness}). We also added the key-uniqueness query, and the six
freshness mutants, which check that every guard is needed. All queries
remained provable. The sketch's ephemeral reveal was adaptive while the
model's is static, and the claims now say so.

\paragraph{Evidence that was weaker than it looked.} The first version of the
$\ct$ mutant showed only that the full proof script aborted once $\ct$ was
removed from $\THH$. That shows the script depends on $\ct$, not that
agreement does. The mutant now runs an authentication-only proof, on the
real model as a control and on the mutant, and checks the agreement queries
directly (\cref{sec:mutations}). The review also asked for the composition
side conditions to be listed; they are in \cref{sec:status}.

\subsection{In the implementation: low-order X25519 points}\label{sec:lowcaught}

X-Wing~\cite{XWingDraft} takes X25519 from RFC~7748~\cite[\S5]{RFC7748} and
applies no check to its output. Encapsulate and Decapsulate
(\S5.4--5.5 of the draft) pass the raw Diffie--Hellman value to the
combiner, and the draft gives Decapsulate no failure case. Our
implementation computed X25519 with pyca/cryptography (version 50.0.1),
which raises \texttt{ValueError} when the output is all-zero. As a result,
our X-Wing decapsulation raised on any ciphertext whose X25519 part is a
low-order point, and encapsulation raised on any such public key, where the
specification derives a key.

Neither the models nor the known-answer tests could see this. The models
treat X25519 as a prime-order group, which has no low-order points. The
draft's test vectors use honestly generated values, which are never
low-order. The divergence did not affect secrecy. Honest parties never
produce these points, and whether a point is low-order is a function of
public data, so the error gave an adversary nothing it could not compute
itself. It was a conformance difference: a peer following the specification
derives a key where ours raised.

\paragraph{The fix.} For clamped scalars, the X25519 output is all-zero
exactly when the peer's u-coordinate, decoded as RFC~7748 prescribes (top bit
masked, value reduced mod $p$), is one of five values. These are $0$, $1$,
$p-1$, and the u-coordinates of the two pairs of points of order~8.
\Cref{app:loworder} proves the characterisation complete: seven integers
below $2^{255}$ represent these residues, giving fourteen 32-byte encodings.
The implementation now catches pyca's error and returns the RFC~7748 value,
32 zero bytes, only if the peer's input decodes to one of the five values;
any other error is raised again:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python,morekeywords={try,except,raise,def,return,if,in}]
def _x25519_dh(sk, peer_pk):
    priv = x25519.X25519PrivateKey.from_private_bytes(sk)
    peer = x25519.X25519PublicKey.from_public_bytes(peer_pk)
    try:
        return priv.exchange(peer)
    except ValueError:
        if _is_low_order(peer_pk):    # public input only
            return bytes(32)          # the RFC 7748 output
        raise
\end{lstlisting}
The ML-KEM-1024 profile keeps the check, because RFC~9180 requires DHKEM
recipients to abort on an all-zero output~\cite[\S7.1.4]{RFC9180}. Its models
do not include that rejection either, but the same argument applies: it
depends only on public data.

\paragraph{Tests.} Sixty-three new tests cover the fix, and \cref{app:loworder}
describes them in detail. An independent RFC~7748 Montgomery ladder,
checked against RFC~7748's test vectors and against pyca on random inputs,
confirms that each of the fourteen encodings gives an all-zero output.
Counting roots over $\Fp$ confirms that no other u-coordinate has order
dividing~8. pyca rejects each encoding, so the fallback is reachable, and it
accepts random and near-miss inputs, so the fallback is not taken
spuriously. Decapsulation and encapsulation return the draft's value,
recomputed with \texttt{hashlib} rather than through the code under test,
and the ML-KEM-1024 profile still rejects. Nine mutants of the fix each make
some of these tests fail. They remove the fallback, catch every error, drop
one point from the set, skip the top-bit mask or the reduction mod $p$,
revert either direction, return a wrong constant, or apply the fallback to
the RFC~9180 leg as well. The full test suite (335 tests) passes.

\subsection{Lessons}\label{sec:lessons}

\paragraph{Mechanize the primitive lemmas, not only the protocol.} Both
errors in the sketch were in ``secure if either component is'' lemmas,
written as a minimum of two advantages without side assumptions. Statements
of that shape read as obviously true. In both cases the missing assumption
appeared as soon as the construction was modelled byte-for-byte as
specified: the composite's message representative, and X-Wing's choice of
combiner inputs.

\paragraph{A checked proof does not check its description.} Every review
finding concerned something we said about the models: how tight the bound
is, what an assumption is called, how strong the adversary is. CryptoVerif
checks none of these. Claims should be read off the bound's terms and the
library macros' definitions, and checked against the models by someone who
did not write them.

\paragraph{Make negative controls test the property, not the script.} A
proof script that aborts after a mutation is weak evidence, since scripts
abort for many reasons. A mutant should check that the query it targets is
no longer proved, next to a control in which the same proof succeeds on the
unmutated model.

\paragraph{Test at the abstraction boundary.} The implementation diverged
exactly where the models abstract (low-order points do not exist in a
prime-order group) and where the test vectors do not reach (they are honest
values). Tests derived from the models' abstractions, rather than from the
specification's happy path, are the ones that find such divergences.
