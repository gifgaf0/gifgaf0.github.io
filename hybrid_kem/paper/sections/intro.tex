\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Hybrid post-quantum/traditional (PQ/T) key exchange pairs a post-quantum KEM
such as ML-KEM~\cite{FIPS203} with a Diffie--Hellman group, so that the
result stays secure as long as either one does. X-Wing~\cite{XWing,XWingDraft}
fixes one such pairing, ML-KEM-768 with X25519, and proves it IND-CCA secure;
generic KEM combiners~\cite{GHP18,BBFGS19} cover other pairings; and composite
signatures~\cite{Composite} pair ML-DSA~\cite{FIPS204} with EdDSA~\cite{RFC8032}
in the same spirit. A protocol built from these primitives inherits their
guarantees only through a composition argument, and that argument is usually
made on paper.

This paper reports a machine-checked computational analysis, in
CryptoVerif~\cite{Bla08,CryptoVerif}, of HYBRID-AKE: a small SIGMA-like
handshake~\cite{Kra03} that derives its key from an ephemeral hybrid KEM and
authenticates with composite signatures over the transcript, with no MAC. We
mechanize both layers. The protocol proof assumes one IND-CCA2 KEM and one
UF-CMA signature; separate proofs show that each hybrid primitive meets that
assumption if either of its components does. The protocol proof and the
post-quantum legs use only CryptoVerif's post-quantum-sound
library~\cite{BJ24}.

The protocol is not the point. It is an engineering design, close to TLS~1.3
and SIGMA, and it is not standardised. What we report is what mechanization,
negative controls and review caught, at three levels:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{The proof sketch.} A pen-and-paper sketch preceded the
  mechanization. Two of its four results were stated without a condition
  they need. The composite signature is unforgeable when either component is
  only if its pre-hash is also collision resistant. X-Wing is secure relying
  on X25519 only if ML-KEM is also ciphertext second-preimage resistant
  (C2PRI). Both conditions are stated in the primary sources, the
  composite-signature draft~\cite{Composite} and the X-Wing paper's
  classical theorem~\cite{XWing}; the sketch had dropped them. Its
  authentication bound also lacked a factor for the number of parties.
  \item \emph{Our own write-up.} A review of the first version of the
  mechanization, by a separate instance of the AI assistant used throughout
  this work, found claims that the models did not support: a tighter bound
  than proved, non-standard assumptions not flagged as such, and two
  restrictions on the adversary that the write-up disclosed but the
  statements of the results did not carry. A second review, of a draft of this paper,
  found that we had also overstated the coverage of our mutation tests.
  \item \emph{The implementation.} The first review also noted that
  pyca/cryptography raises an error on an all-zero X25519 output, which the
  models cannot express. Tests confirmed that our X-Wing implementation
  raised an error on low-order X25519 points, where the X-Wing
  specification's algorithm returns a shared secret. The same divergence had
  been reported for another library~\cite{Noble26}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Machine-checked proofs (9 models, 17 queries, CryptoVerif~2.13, under
  20~seconds in total) of: injective agreement in both directions;
  real-or-random secrecy of every fresh session key, with forward secrecy,
  key-compromise impersonation resistance and key reveal; cross-role key
  uniqueness; the same results under static ephemeral-key reveal; IND-CCA2 of
  X-Wing relying on either leg; IND-CCA2 of an ML-KEM-1024/X25519
  transcript-hashing combiner relying on either leg, including a
  random-oracle proof from standard assumptions; and EUF-CMA of the composite
  signature relying on either component (\cref{sec:model,sec:mech}).
  \item Twenty-two mutation tests, each deleting one design element,
  assumption or model guard, with controls (\cref{sec:mutations}). Twelve of
  them remove the twelve freshness and RevealKey guards of the protocol
  model one at a time. They show that the proofs depend on the KEM
  ciphertext and the responder's key being in the signed transcript, that
  every freshness guard has an effect, that X-Wing's proof needs C2PRI where
  the transcript-hashing combiner's does not, and that the role-separating
  context strings are redundant given the encodings.
  \item A catalogue of the findings above, with their causes and fixes, and
  the lessons we draw from them (\cref{sec:caught}).
  \item For the implementation finding, a fix, a self-contained proof of the
  known list of X25519 inputs that produce an all-zero output, and tests
  that check it against an independent RFC~7748 implementation and by
  counting roots over $\Fp$, with nine mutants of the fix that the tests must
  catch (\cref{sec:lowcaught,app:loworder}).
\end{itemize}
All models, proof scripts, mutation tests and implementation
tests are public~\cite{Artifact}, and the sources of this paper are in the
same repository.

To our knowledge, these are the first machine-checked proofs of X-Wing's
IND-CCA2 security relying on either leg, stated as multi-user KEM
equivalences, and of the EUF-CMA security of the draft composite
ML-DSA/EdDSA signatures. We claim no novelty for the conditions themselves
(C2PRI and the collision-resistant pre-hash), for the list of low-order
points, or for the per-role secrecy pattern, which comes from CryptoVerif's
TLS~1.3 analysis~\cite{BBK17}. The closest mechanized work covers generic KEM
combiners and KEM-based hybrid key exchanges in Squirrel~\cite{BDDJK26},
PQXDH~\cite{BJKS24}, and the hybrid TLS~1.3 and SSH key exchanges in
CryptoVerif~\cite{BJ24} (\cref{sec:related}).

\paragraph{How this work was done.}
This work was AI-assisted throughout. As the repository history shows, the
protocol specification, its implementation, the Tamarin models and the
pen-and-paper sketch were written with an AI assistant (Claude, by
Anthropic) at the author's direction, as were the CryptoVerif models, proof
scripts, tests and a first draft of this paper. Both reviews described here
were carried out by separate instances of the same assistant. They could
read the repository and its history but not the development conversation.
We state this because it bears on how the results should be weighed. The
errors of \cref{sec:sketchcaught} are errors in an AI-written sketch, caught
by an AI-assisted mechanization. The machine-checked statements rest on
CryptoVerif's soundness and on the models matching the specification. The
reviews, by contrast, are not a substitute for expert peer review
(\cref{sec:limits}).
