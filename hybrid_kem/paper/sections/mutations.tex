\section{Mutation tests}\label{sec:mutations}

A proved query can be weaker than it looks. The model may not depend on the
design element it is meant to justify, an oracle meant to help the adversary
may never fire, or an assumption may be stronger than needed. Symbolic tools
answer part of this with reachability lemmas; our Tamarin model, for
instance, proves that an honest run exists. CryptoVerif has no such lemmas,
so we use mutation tests. Each test deletes one design element or
assumption, runs CryptoVerif again with the same proof, and checks the
outcome against an expectation fixed in advance: the dependent query must
fail, and, where stated, the other queries must still be proved. Two
\emph{controls} must stay proved. A script, \file{mutations.py}, applies the
edits to fresh copies of the models; \texttt{make mutations} runs all sixteen
tests in about a minute. \Cref{tab:mutations} lists them, and all sixteen
behave as expected.

\paragraph{What they show.} The protocol mutants identify the transcript
fields that authentication depends on. Without $\ct$ in $\THH$, a man in the
middle can replace the responder's ciphertext with its own encapsulation to
$\pke$, and both agreement queries fail. Without $\vk_R$, the initiator's
$m_3$ no longer commits to the responder it accepted. Under plain UF-CMA, a
signature can verify under a second, adversarial key, so initiator
authentication is not proved. The two context strings turn out to be a
second, redundant layer of role separation. The proofs survive their removal
because the two signed encodings already have different field counts, and
they fail only when both separations go.

The six freshness mutants are vacuity checks on the model itself. Each
removes one guard of the freshness definition or of RevealKey, and the
corresponding secrecy query then fails. A guard whose removal changed
nothing would mean that the reveal oracle never fires in that situation, and
the secrecy result would hold for a weaker adversary than described. The
mutants check that each guard is needed, not that it is as weak as it could
be. The first version of the model refused reveals more often than
necessary, and these mutants would not have caught that
(\cref{sec:reviewcaught}).

The primitive mutants show the assumptions are necessary to the proofs:
collision resistance of the pre-hash for the composite, and C2PRI for
X-Wing. Together, the last two mutants make the contrast between the
combiners concrete. X-Wing's X25519-direction proof needs C2PRI because the
combiner omits $\ct_M$. The ML-KEM-1024 combiner needs no property of ML-KEM
at all, but only because it hashes $\ct_M$; once $\ct_M$ is removed, its
proof fails too.

\paragraph{What they do not show.} CryptoVerif is sound but not complete,
so a failed proof is not by itself an attack. For the $\ct$, pre-hash and
C2PRI mutants there is a concrete attack, sketched above and in
\cref{sec:mech:kem}. For the others, the failure shows only that the proof
uses the element, not that every proof must. The mutants also say nothing
about elements we did not think to remove.
