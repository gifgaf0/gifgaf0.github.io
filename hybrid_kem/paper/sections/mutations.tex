\section{Mutation tests}\label{sec:mutations}

A proved query can be weaker than it looks. The model may not depend on the
design element it is meant to justify, an oracle meant to help the adversary
may never fire, or an assumption may be stronger than needed. Symbolic tools
answer part of this with reachability lemmas; our Tamarin model, for
instance, proves that an honest run exists. CryptoVerif has no such lemmas,
so we use mutation tests. Each test deletes one design element, assumption
or model guard, runs CryptoVerif again, and checks the outcome against an
expectation fixed in advance: the dependent query must fail, and, where
stated, named other queries must still be proved. All but three tests reuse
the model's own proof; the three authentication-only tests replace it with
the signature step alone, which proves both agreement queries on the
unmutated model (the first control). A script, \file{mutations.py}, applies
the edits to fresh copies of the models, and \texttt{make mutations} runs all
twenty-two tests in about two minutes. \Cref{tab:mutations} lists them, and
all twenty-two behave as expected.

\paragraph{What they show.} The protocol mutants identify the transcript
fields that the authentication proofs depend on. Without $\ct$ in $\THH$, a
man in the middle can replace the responder's ciphertext with its own
encapsulation to $\pke$, and both agreement queries fail. Without $\vk_R$,
the initiator's $m_3$ no longer commits to the responder it accepted. Under
plain UF-CMA, a signature can verify under a second, adversarial key, so
initiator authentication is not proved. The two context strings turn out to
be a second, redundant layer of role separation. The proofs survive their
removal because the two signed encodings already have different field
counts, and they fail only when both separations go.

The twelve freshness mutants are vacuity checks on the model itself. The
protocol model has twelve guards that decide which keys are tested and which
may be revealed: four for initiators (honest peer, peer uncorrupted at
acceptance, and no revealed partner of either responder kind), three for
mutual responders, and five in the three RevealKey oracles. Each mutant
removes exactly one, and the corresponding secrecy query then fails. A guard
whose removal changed nothing would mean that the guard is dead code, and
the secrecy result would hold for a weaker adversary than described. The
mutants check that each guard is needed, not that it is as weak as it could
be. The first version of the model refused reveals more often than
necessary, and mutants like these would not have caught that
(\cref{sec:reviewcaught}).

The primitive mutants show that the assumptions are necessary to the
proofs: collision resistance of the pre-hash for the composite, and C2PRI for
X-Wing. Together, the last two mutants make the contrast between the
combiners concrete. X-Wing's X25519-direction proof needs C2PRI because the
combiner omits $\ct_M$. The ML-KEM-1024 combiner's proof needs no security
assumption on ML-KEM, but only because it hashes $\ct_M$; once $\ct_M$ is
removed, its proof fails too.

\paragraph{What they do not show.} CryptoVerif is sound but not complete,
so a failed proof is not by itself an attack. For the $\ct$, pre-hash and
C2PRI mutants there is a concrete attack, sketched above and in
\cref{sec:mech:kem}. For the others, the failure shows only that the proof
uses the element, not that every proof must. The mutants also say nothing
about elements we did not think to remove. Indeed, until the review of a
draft of this paper, our suite covered only seven of the twelve guards, two
of them only together (\cref{sec:reviewcaught}).
