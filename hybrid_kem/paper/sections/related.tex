\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{X-Wing and KEM combiners.} The X-Wing paper~\cite{XWing} proves
X-Wing IND-CCA secure classically, from strong Diffie--Hellman in a nominal
group with SHA3-256 as a random oracle and C2PRI of
ML-KEM~\cite[Thm.~1]{XWing}, and post-quantum, from IND-CCA of ML-KEM and
SHA3-256 as a PRF in the standard model~\cite[Thm.~2]{XWing}. It also
proves ML-KEM-768 C2PRI with its hash functions as random
oracles~\cite[Thm.~3]{XWing}. Our KEM models prove analogues of the first
two theorems as CryptoVerif equivalences, with X25519 as a prime-order group
and ML-KEM perfectly correct. Connolly et al.\ show that X-Wing's combiner applies
to other KEMs, ML-KEM-1024 among them~\cite{Starfighters}. Generic KEM
combiners and their security were studied by Giacon, Heuer and
Poettering~\cite{GHP18}, who introduced split-key PRFs, and by Bindel et
al.~\cite{BBFGS19}, whose transcript-hashing combiner our ML-KEM-1024
profile follows. Millerjord, Stebila and Steckel study split-key PRFs
further and give a standard-model construction~\cite{MSS26}. Binding
properties of KEMs, including those ML-KEM lacks, were systematised by
Cremers, Dax and Medinger~\cite{CDM24} and by Schmieg~\cite{Sch24}. Kang,
Lee and Son~\cite{KLS26} study which public values a hybrid KEM's combiner
must hash, taking X-Wing as their case study.

\paragraph{Mechanized analyses of hybrid and post-quantum protocols.}
Blanchet and Jacomme made CryptoVerif post-quantum sound and used it to
verify hybrid TLS~1.3 and SSH key exchanges~\cite{BJ24}; our protocol proof
uses their library. Bhargavan et al.\ analysed Signal's PQXDH, which
combines X25519 with a post-quantum KEM, in ProVerif and CryptoVerif, found
flaws in its specification, and proved a binding property of the KEM it
uses~\cite{BJKS24}. Baelde et al.\ mechanize post-quantum proofs in Squirrel
for four KEM combiners and two KEM-based hybrid key exchanges. They point
out that post-quantum CryptoVerif has no quantum values, which makes it hard
to extend to settings such as the quantum random-oracle
model~\cite{BDDJK26}. In our development, random oracles appear only in the
X25519 directions, whose assumptions are classical anyway. Earlier CryptoVerif case studies
include TLS~1.3 drafts~\cite{BBK17}, from which we take the per-role secrecy
pattern; HPKE~\cite{ABHKLR21}, which introduced nominal groups and the
KEM-equivalence proof pattern that our KEM models follow; and
WireGuard~\cite{LBB19}, with a cofactor-aware X25519 model. Our protocol is
closer in shape to SIGMA~\cite{Kra03} and TLS~1.3's signature-authenticated
handshake~\cite{DFGS21} than to KEM-authenticated designs such as
KEMTLS~\cite{SSW20}. We are not aware of an earlier machine-checked proof of
X-Wing itself or of composite ML-DSA/EdDSA signatures.

\paragraph{Errors found by mechanization.} CV2EC~\cite{CV2EC} translates
CryptoVerif assumptions into EasyCrypt so that they can be proved from
standard assumptions. Doing so, its authors found two errors in the
pen-and-paper proof of the HPKE analysis's $n$-user result for authenticated
KEMs, a counting error and a missing public-key collision term, which the
HPKE authors then corrected. They also found that an assumption stated in a
CryptoVerif model leaked the algorithms' randomness to the adversary, so
that no reasonable KEM could satisfy it. Oechsner, Pereira and Scholl show
the converse risk, a mechanized proof that was itself flawed and allowed an
attack on a formally verified implementation~\cite{OPS25}. Our findings are
milder, but of the same two kinds: conditions that the pen-and-paper sketch
dropped, and claims about a mechanization that went beyond it.

\paragraph{Hybrid signatures.} Bindel et al.\ study combiners for hybrid
signatures, including how strongly the components are bound
together~\cite{BHMS17}. The composite-signature draft states that the
composite is EUF-CMA if at least one component is and the pre-hash is
collision resistant~\cite[\S1.3]{Composite}. \Cref{lem:sig} is a
machine-checked version of that statement.

\paragraph{Low-order points.} Cremers and Jackson analyse small-subgroup and
invalid-curve attacks on protocols that use Diffie--Hellman, and model such
groups in Tamarin~\cite{CJ19}. The five low-order u-coordinates of
Curve25519 and its twist are well known; Mbed~TLS, for example, rejects
exactly these values~\cite{mbedtls}. The specifications disagree on the
all-zero
X25519 output. RFC~7748 lets implementations check for it~\cite{RFC7748};
RFC~9180 requires DHKEM recipients to abort on it~\cite{RFC9180}; RFC~10024
requires the check for hybrid key agreement in TLS~1.3~\cite{RFC10024}; and
X-Wing specifies none~\cite{XWingDraft}. Libraries whose X25519 functions
reject the all-zero output therefore diverge from X-Wing's algorithm unless
they compensate, as reported for noble~\cite{Noble26} and found in our own
implementation (\cref{sec:lowcaught}).

\paragraph{Sanity checks.} Vacuity, a property that holds for a trivial
reason, is a known hazard in model checking~\cite{Kup06}. Symbolic protocol
tools address part of it with reachability lemmas. Our symbolic Tamarin model
of this protocol proves that an honest run exists, and it caught a modelling
error of its own when an under-specified honest-peer premise made an
agreement lemma fail. For computational models, the vacuous CryptoVerif
assumption found by CV2EC~\cite{CV2EC} shows the same hazard, and the
mutation tests of \cref{sec:mutations} are one way to look for it. We did not
find earlier systematic mutation testing of CryptoVerif or EasyCrypt models.
