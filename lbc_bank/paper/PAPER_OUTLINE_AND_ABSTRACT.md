# Paper — outline and abstract

**Plain-language summary.** If empty space were a material, matter and light would both be ripples or knots in it, and they would need to obey the same speed limit. One picture of this kind makes the material a *supersolid* (a crystal that is also a superfluid), with light as its shear wave and particles as knotted whirlpools. We show that a supersolid has a second sound wave, that the whirlpools are coupled to it through their cores and through their own flow, and that nothing ties its speed to the light speed. In a standard model supersolid it is slower than light, so fast matter would make a sonic boom and lose energy, unless the two speeds happened to agree to about one part in 10²³ or the coupling were tuned almost to zero. We compute this in that model in two and three dimensions, and in two dimensions all the way up to the point where it melts. The second sound always stays below the light speed. The argument does not apply to a nearly incompressible, mostly superfluid crystal, or near a continuous melting transition, where second sound can outrun light. The general worry is known in the emergent-gravity literature; what is new is this concrete supersolid calculation and its numbers.

**Working thesis, as tested.** *Supported:* matter's drag-free range ends at the slowest sound it couples to, light's speed is the shear speed, and no symmetry ties the two together. *Supported, derived twice* (`vortex_coupling_check.py` and the blind `vortex_coupling_second_leg/`): a moving vortex reaches the density through its core deficit and, independently, through its own flow (the co-moving Bernoulli term, whose velocity part the circulation quantum fixes), and it reaches shear through the normal fraction. *Supported:* in the measured regime (c₂ < c_T: the whole stable supersolid phase of the model in 2D, and the 3D crystal computed) fast matter radiates into the vacuum. *Not realized in this model:* the regime c₂ > c_T. The first-order melting transition intervenes first (c₂/c_T ≤ 0.77 in the stable phase and ≤ 0.79 on the metastable branch, where it then falls as second sound softens toward the crystal's spinodal; both values from two independent computations). Hydrodynamics allows that regime near a continuous transition, or in a nearly incompressible, isotropic 3D supersolid with superfluid fraction above 3/4 (Green's limit, derived twice: `green_limit_check.py` and `green_limit_second_leg/`). There every mode that matter couples to is at least as fast as light, so the argument does not apply, to dislocations or to vortices. *Withdrawn on October 4, after an outside review:* "a vortex still drives second sound through its circulation in Green's limit" and "vortex matter could then outrun light"; the derivation supports neither. *Supported:* a common cone needs 1 − c₂/c_T ≲ 1/2Γ² ≈ 5×10⁻²⁴ for the most energetic cosmic rays (Γ the Lorentz factor), or every coupling of matter to second sound about 19 orders of magnitude weaker than computed.

**Prior-art status.** The general statement (multiple sound speeds give multiple metrics, so a common light cone needs fine tuning, and subluminal coupled modes are Cherenkov-radiated) is published: Barceló, Liberati and Visser (2002, 2005/2011); Visser and Weinfurtner (2005); Liberati, Visser and Weinfurtner (2006); Collins et al. (2004); Anber and Donoghue (2011); Coleman and Glashow (1997, 1999); Moore and Nelson (2001); Elliott, Moore and Stoica (2005). The soft-core supersolid's branch ordering c₂ < c_T < c₁ is in Poli, Baillie, Ferlaino and Blakie (2024). The vacuum-as-supersolid picture with light as shear and matter as defects (chiefly dislocations) exists only as a non-peer-reviewed preprint (M. A. Cox 2026, Zenodo), which asserts that matter moves without drag. Its v4 body, read in full on October 4, 2026 (`../lit/COX_V4_BODY_READ.md`), never treats second sound, the Landau velocity or Cherenkov drag. It keeps a single longitudinal sound at about 10²⁰ c (Green's route), and at its own superfluid fraction (4/5) our hydrodynamics puts its omitted second sound above the light speed. The paper is therefore framed as **a concrete, quantified instance of the known fine-tuning problem in the supersolid class, and a direct test, in the computed model, of the claim that superfluidity removes drag.** It does not claim to refute the Cosserat proposal.

## Title

**Second sound breaks the common light cone of a supersolid vacuum**

## Abstract

Medium-based models of the vacuum must give matter and light one causal cone: every excitation that matter couples to must be at least as fast as light. A supersolid is the natural medium for one picture of this kind: its superfluid order hosts vortex-knot "matter" and its shear rigidity carries transverse "light". A supersolid, however, has two longitudinal sounds. Zero-temperature supersolid hydrodynamics gives the fraction of the density response carried by the lower one (second sound), F₋ = ρ_s(M − ργ)² / [ρ²ρ_n(c₊² − c_*²)(c₊² − c₋²)], which vanishes only without superflow or if two independent elastic coefficients coincide, M = ργ. Bogoliubov–de Gennes calculations for the soft-core Gross–Pitaevskii supersolid in two and three dimensions, checked against two sum rules and an independent static route, put second sound at 0.06–0.8 of the shear speed, with 0.2–41 % of the f-sum and 64–80 % of the static response. Toward melting (computed in two dimensions) c₂/c_T rises to 0.77 at the first-order phase boundary and peaks at 0.79 on the metastable branch; it never reaches one. A moving vortex drives the density through its core and, independently, through its own flow, with a strength fixed by the circulation quantum, so fast matter radiates second sound, with a drag proportional to that branch's f-sum share. Ultra-high-energy cosmic rays then survive only if defects are kilometres across (a proton-size one falls short by about 19 orders of magnitude), if second sound is tuned to within 10⁻²³ of the shear speed or its coupling to matter made 19 orders weaker, or if it is faster than shear. Hydrodynamics allows that near a continuous melting transition, or in Green's limit of the elastic ether: a nearly incompressible, isotropic three-dimensional supersolid with superfluid fraction above 3/4. This is a concrete instance of the fine-tuning problem of emergent Lorentz invariance, with roots in the nineteenth-century elastic-ether debate.

## Outline

1. **Introduction.** Requirement: a common cone, with every coupled mode at least as fast as light. The mono-metricity problem in analogue gravity and its observational bite (maximal attainable velocities; gravitational Cherenkov). Medium vacua: superfluid, crystal, elastic-duality. The supersolid picture and its no-drag claim. Frank–Eshelby defect kinematics in ordinary crystals, where shear is the slowest sound. What we add.
2. **Why a supersolid.** Vortex matter needs condensate phase order; transverse light needs shear rigidity. The soft-core model. Three gapless branches.
3. **Longitudinal response of a supersolid.** The T = 0 Lagrangian; χ(q, ω) as a two-pole form; f-sum shares; the closed form for F₋ and its two zeros; compressibility sum rule; static shares.
4. **Numbers for the soft-core supersolid.** Method (cell GP, BdG density matrix elements, two sum rules, mode identification by polarization); the canonical 2D state; the static hydrodynamic route; the interaction sweep and a second kernel; the 3D close-packed stack. Table 1, Table 2.
5. **Approach to melting.** The fixed-density coexistence boundary by common tangent; c₂/c_T and F₂ up to and beyond it; comparison with the literature ordering; why c₂ > c_T is not excluded in principle (including the Green's-limit route: f_s > 3/4 in 3D).
6. **How a vortex couples to the medium.** Core deficits (single-component and filled-core cases); the flow vertex from the Galilean-invariant superfluid term (the co-moving Bernoulli pressure; its velocity part is fixed by the circulation quantum, and for a ring it exceeds the deficit vertex by an order of magnitude); the transverse force on the lattice through ρ_n (shear Cherenkov above c_T); the density and strain dependence of ρ_n (not treated). Derived twice.
7. **Drag, radiation and loss length.** Landau criterion with several branches (for a vortex, min(c₂, c_T)); the drag formula (∝ F_ν; speed-independent for an isotropic point source); multipole radiation scaling (≈10¹¹ in favour of second sound); an order-of-magnitude loss length (a proton-size defect about 19 orders short; two corrections that roughly cancel); the cosmic-ray bound; the coupling route (M = ργ to 10⁻⁹, or the incompressible limit, neither of which removes a strain coupling).
8. **Binding and drag.** The static induced attraction and its slow-branch share; the vortex's flow vertex radiates but does not bind.
9. **Escapes, and the elastic-ether debate.** Green's incompressible limit (F₋ → 0; a counterflow mode with no density and no mass current remains; with constant ρ_n a vortex whose core shrinks with the healing length no longer reaches it, but a fixed core deficit or a strain coupling does; f_s > 3/4 puts it above shear, and the argument does not apply); Cauchy and Kelvin's labile ether; MacCullagh's rotational medium, the exception, and why vortex matter cannot use it; the Cosserat-supersolid proposal (Green's route; at its own superfluid fraction its omitted second sound is faster than light, so its matter, limited to the shear speed, escapes the drag).
10. **Discussion.** Relation to the known fine-tuning problem; limitations (mean field, one kernel family, linear response); the open question (can a real supersolid reach c₂ > c_T?); the Lorentz-invariant soliton alternative.
