# Paper — outline and abstract

**Plain-language summary.** If empty space were a material, matter and light would both be ripples or knots in it, and they would need to obey the same speed limit. A popular version makes the material a *supersolid* (a crystal that is also a superfluid), with light as its shear wave and particles as knotted whirlpools. We show that a supersolid has a second sound wave that the whirlpools are coupled to, and that nothing ties its speed to the light speed. In a standard model supersolid it is slower than light, so fast matter would make a sonic boom and lose energy, unless the two speeds happened to agree to about one part in 10²³. We compute this in that model, in two and three dimensions, all the way up to the point where it melts. The second sound always stays below the light speed. The general worry is known in the emergent-gravity literature; what is new is this concrete supersolid calculation and its numbers.

**Working thesis, as tested.** *Supported:* matter's drag-free range ends at the slowest longitudinal sound its cores couple to, light's speed is the shear speed, and no symmetry ties the two together. *Supported:* in the measured regime (c₂ < c_T, which is the whole stable supersolid phase of the model) fast matter radiates into the vacuum. *Not realized in this model:* the regime c₂ > c_T ("matter could outrun light"). The first-order melting transition intervenes first (c₂/c_T ≤ 0.77 in the stable phase and ≤ 0.79 on the metastable branch, where it then falls as second sound softens toward the crystal's spinodal; both values from two independent computations). Hydrodynamics does not forbid that regime near a continuous transition, or in a nearly incompressible, isotropic 3D supersolid with superfluid fraction above 3/4 (Green's limit, derived twice: `green_limit_check.py` and `green_limit_second_leg/`). There it frees matter whose limiting speed is the shear speed (such as dislocations), but vortex matter could then outrun light. So it stays a model-dependent possibility, and it does not help vortex-knot matter. *Supported:* a common cone needs c₂ = c_T to extraordinary precision (|1 − c₂/c_T| ≲ 1/2γ² ≈ 5×10⁻²⁴ for the most energetic cosmic rays).

**Prior-art status.** The general statement (multiple sound speeds give multiple metrics, so a common light cone needs fine tuning, and subluminal coupled modes are Cherenkov-radiated) is published: Barceló, Liberati and Visser (2002, 2005/2011); Visser and Weinfurtner (2005); Liberati, Visser and Weinfurtner (2006); Collins et al. (2004); Anber and Donoghue (2011); Coleman and Glashow (1997, 1999); Moore and Nelson (2001); Elliott, Moore and Stoica (2005). The soft-core supersolid's branch ordering c₂ < c_T < c₁ is in Poli, Baillie, Ferlaino and Blakie (2024). The vacuum-as-supersolid picture with light as shear and matter as defects (chiefly dislocations) exists only as a non-peer-reviewed preprint (M. A. Cox 2026, Zenodo), which asserts that matter moves without drag. Its v4 body, read in full on October 4, 2026 (`../lit/COX_V4_BODY_READ.md`), never treats second sound, the Landau velocity or Cherenkov drag. It keeps a single longitudinal sound at about 10²⁰ c (Green's route), and at its own superfluid fraction (4/5) our hydrodynamics puts its omitted second sound above the light speed. The paper is therefore framed as **a concrete, quantified instance of the known fine-tuning problem in the supersolid class, and a direct test, in the computed model, of the claim that superfluidity removes drag.** It does not claim to refute the Cosserat proposal.

## Title

**Second sound breaks the common light cone of a supersolid vacuum**

## Abstract

Medium-based models of the vacuum must give matter and light one causal cone, and every excitation that matter couples to must be at least as fast as light. A supersolid is the natural medium for one popular picture: its superfluid order hosts vortex-knot "matter" and its shear rigidity carries transverse "light". We show that this picture fails generically. A supersolid has two longitudinal sounds. From zero-temperature supersolid hydrodynamics we obtain the fraction of the density response carried by the lower one (second sound), F₋ = ρ_s(M − ργ)² / [ρ²ρ_n(c₊² − c_*²)(c₊² − c₋²)]. It vanishes only without superflow or under the coincidence M = ργ between two independent elastic coefficients. We run Bogoliubov–de Gennes calculations for the soft-core Gross–Pitaevskii supersolid in two and three dimensions, checked against the f-sum and compressibility sum rules and against an independent static hydrodynamic route. Second sound comes out at 0.06–0.8 of the shear speed, carrying 0.2–41 % of the f-sum and 64–80 % of the static density response. Toward the first-order melting transition the ratio c₂/c_T rises to 0.77 at the phase boundary and to 0.79 on the metastable crystal branch, then falls as second sound softens toward the crystal's spinodal; it never reaches one. Vortex cores carry density deficits and circulation, so moving matter radiates second sound above c₂ < c_T. The drag is proportional to the branch's f-sum share and independent of its speed, and the same vertex supplies the short-range static attraction between defects. Survival of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays then requires a macroscopic substrate scale (≳ 10⁴ m) or |1 − c₂/c_T| ≲ 10⁻²³. The result is a concrete instance of the fine-tuning problem of emergent Lorentz invariance, and we connect it to the nineteenth-century elastic-ether debate.

## Outline

1. **Introduction.** Requirement: a common cone, with every coupled mode at least as fast as light. The mono-metricity problem in analogue gravity and its observational bite (maximal attainable velocities; gravitational Cherenkov). Medium vacua: superfluid, crystal, elastic-duality. The supersolid picture and its no-drag claim. Frank–Eshelby defect kinematics in ordinary crystals, where shear is the slowest sound. What we add.
2. **Why a supersolid.** Vortex matter needs condensate phase order; transverse light needs shear rigidity. The soft-core model. Three gapless branches.
3. **Longitudinal response of a supersolid.** The T = 0 Lagrangian; χ(q, ω) as a two-pole form; f-sum shares; the closed form for F₋ and its two zeros; compressibility sum rule; static shares.
4. **Numbers for the soft-core supersolid.** Method (cell GP, BdG density matrix elements, two sum rules, mode identification by polarization); the canonical 2D state; the static hydrodynamic route; the interaction sweep and a second kernel; the 3D close-packed stack. Table 1, Table 2.
5. **Approach to melting.** The fixed-density coexistence boundary by common tangent; c₂/c_T and F₂ up to and beyond it; comparison with the literature ordering; why c₂ > c_T is not excluded in principle (including the Green's-limit route: f_s > 3/4 in 3D).
6. **Why defects couple to second sound.** Core deficits (single-component and filled-core cases); circulation and the phase character of second sound.
7. **Drag, radiation and loss length.** Landau criterion with several branches; the drag formula (∝ F_ν, speed-independent); multipole radiation scaling; an order-of-magnitude loss length; the cosmic-ray bound and the required tuning.
8. **Binding and drag share a vertex.** The static induced attraction and its slow-branch share.
9. **Escapes, and the elastic-ether debate.** Green's incompressible limit (F₋ → 0, but a density-free counterflow mode remains); Cauchy and Kelvin's labile ether; MacCullagh's rotational medium, the exception, and why vortex matter cannot use it; the Cosserat-supersolid proposal (Green's route; at its own superfluid fraction its omitted second sound is faster than light, so its matter, limited to the shear speed, escapes the drag, while vortex matter in the same medium would not).
10. **Discussion.** Relation to the known fine-tuning problem; limitations (mean field, one kernel family, linear response); the Lorentz-invariant soliton alternative.
